## The Brief

Dressmaker is a game from Cozy Lives and Free Lives, released in September 2026. You run a dress shop. Story clients hand you a card of requirements, and you design a dress to match. The off-rack dresses, the ones you make for the shop floor, have no card at all. You're on your own.

Tailor-Made makes one up. You get a client, a short brief and a few directions to design towards. You can share a challenge as a link, or play the daily one that everyone gets. There's no backend and no account.

I'm building it for the Dressmaker community first. It's also a deliberate test: can I run a whole project with AI, from planning to a live site, and still own every decision? I planned this case study on day one and documented the process as it happened. Tickets with something worth showing carried a `case-study` label, so the artefacts were there when I needed them.


## Research

Dressmaker was a month old when I started, so there was no API and no data dump. I took my data from a fan wiki, dressmakerwiki.site, which was showing build 410 data. I checked its tag counts against a count I made myself, and read other guide sites to see that they described the same kind of card.

I catalogued all 77 story commission cards in a private note. I wasn't copying them. I wanted their patterns. A card can set a minimum, a ceiling, an avoid, an "only" list, a share or a count. Those patterns became the grammar for Tailor-Made's directions. The card text stays in that private note and nowhere in the app or on this page.

The game gets patched often, so I checked build 443, the latest one with any evidence, before freezing the first data set. It added new fabrics and accessories, but no new fabric type, accessory type, style tag or colour. So I kept the data at the level of fabric types. There are 8 of them, against hundreds of individual fabrics. A generator that says "Lace" or "No Silk" doesn't go stale every time a patch adds a print.

## Dropping the Numbers

The first spec the AI wrote copied the commission cards. A challenge had tag scores, a Quality target, a budget cap and percentages like "at least 40% velvet".

I knew the game, and I knew those numbers don't show up on an off-rack dress. You can't see a tag score or a running cost. A player could never check a challenge like that. It would only work if they trusted the app.

I rewrote the scope before any generator code existed. The new rule is that **every direction must be something you can judge by eye**. That means a style, a fabric, a colour to use or avoid, an accessory to add or avoid, or a small count you can tally. Trim lengths went too, since nobody measures those on screen.

The examples below are invented, not taken from the game:

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Keep the cost under a set budget | No Silk |
| At least 40% velvet | Velvet |
| A minimum score in the Gothic tag | Gothic |
| A set length of trim | At least 5 bows |

The spec also had a cut line, so scope couldn't quietly grow. If briefs and difficulty together turned out to be too much, I'd keep the briefs and drop the difficulty dial. I never needed it, but it was written down before I started.

## Flows Before Style

I drew 26 screens and states, each at desktop (1440px) and mobile (390px), as lo-fi frames in Claude Design. That covers the empty generator, every difficulty, a card with some directions ticked, rerolls, the share link, a bad link, the daily page and the About page.

The grey boxes and sketch type are on purpose. These frames only settle layout and flow. I wasn't choosing a look yet.

## Four Directions, One Pick

Nuzlocke had a moodboard. This one didn't need it. I already had the palette and the atelier feel in my head: warm, dark and a bit old-fashioned, like a sewing room. So I asked Claude Design for four directions. Each one shows a wordmark, a palette, type and the same client card.

I picked Rosewood Label. It felt warm and cosy, the way the game does. The other three were far too high-fashion for the people who play it. I wanted less Chanel, more local independent boutique. That was an audience call, and it was mine. The sheets were all good, but only one of them looked like it belonged to Dressmaker players.

The canvas calls it Rosewood Label. In the repo it became Rosewood Atelier.

## Hi-Fi

I redrew all 26 frames in Rosewood Atelier. The rules of the look are short. It's dark only, with no shadows, and one dusty rose accent carries every button, tick and link.

I didn't eyeball the values from a screenshot. I read the palette, type sizes and component shapes from the canvas markup and wrote them into a design doc, so Claude Code builds to measured tokens and not a guess.

### Decisions win over frames

The hi-fi frames aren't always right. The README for the frames lists where they're wrong, so Claude Code builds to my decisions and not to the drawings.

The clearest case is Chaos. The frame below draws a reroll button on every direction of a six-direction Chaos card. But Chaos can't be rerolled, to keep the difficulty honest. That was my decision, and the drawing got it wrong. The README says so plainly: "Decisions already made win over a frame. Do not build these as drawn."
